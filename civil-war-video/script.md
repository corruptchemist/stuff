## Part 2 — Something Interesting I Learned

**ON SCREEN:** "Part Two" card → free/slave state map → 1856 Frémont "Free Soil, Free Men" banner → Whig collapse graphic

Part two: something I learned that I thought was really cool.

You'd think that anyone who was against slavery wanted to end it. But that's not quite how it worked.

Before this project, I pretty much assumed that being against slavery meant being an abolitionist. But Eric Foner showed me that a lot of Northern Republicans weren't trying to end slavery at all. They just didn't want it spreading west. Their big idea was free labor: a regular person should be able to work, save up, and get ahead, and they worried slavery in the territories would take that chance away. And many of them still didn't believe Black Americans deserved equal rights. So being against slavery's expansion and supporting racial equality were not the same thing.

The other thing that surprised me was how slavery broke the political parties themselves. The Whig Party had supporters in both the North and the South, but its members couldn't agree on slavery, and by the mid-1850s it had basically fallen apart.

So slavery wasn't just causing arguments in Congress. It was reshaping the whole political system, years before the war started.

## Part 3 — How It Affects America Today

**ON SCREEN:** "Part Three" card → 1860 map dissolving into a modern map → 13th/14th/15th Amendment cards → civil rights-era image → U.S. Capitol

Part three: how this still affects America today.

The country has changed a lot since the 1860s, but the most direct connection is the Constitution itself. After the war, the 13th Amendment ended slavery, the 14th made formerly enslaved people citizens and promised equal protection under the law, and the 15th protected the right to vote regardless of race. The 14th Amendment still comes up in major court cases today.

But those promises took a long time to become real, through Reconstruction, the Civil Rights Movement, and the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

Another connection is the debate over federal versus state power, which Americans still argue about. But before the war, that debate was really about slavery. The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 used federal power to make Northern states help return people who had escaped slavery, even when those states were against it. So slaveholders were fine with a strong federal government when it protected slavery.

For me, the biggest lesson was how dangerous division can get when people stop seeing compromise as an option. Today isn't the 1850s, but studying this helped me understand where some of our divisions started.
